Current Agricultural Context & Challenges

Bangladesh's legal framework is anchored in the Pesticide Act of 2018, which mandates registration, licensing, labeling, and safe handling. However, implementation and enforcement remain weak, creating uncertainty for sustainable crop-protection investment.

Critical Challenge: A survey found that 68% of farmers had used pesticides officially banned in Bangladesh, while 93% were unaware of these bans or associated health risks.

Researchers identified 25 highly hazardous pesticides registered for use in Bangladesh according to the FAO. Ten of these are widely used and linked to cancer, kidney failure, heart disease, and reproductive disorders. Insecticide application has surged by more than 80% in five years, yet post-harvest losses still cost an estimated $2.4 billion annually.

Regulatory Reform & Registration Pathways

A key barrier is the absence of a dedicated regulatory framework for biopesticides, creating uncertainty in registration and approval. Complex, time-consuming approval processes deter manufacturers from entering the market.

Short-term Actions

  • Finalize updated Pesticide Rules under the 2018 Act with FAO support.
  • Establish transparent registration with risk-based review.
  • Align national frameworks with ASEAN biocontrol guidelines.

Medium-term Structural Reforms

  • Create a stand-alone biopesticide definition or dedicated chapter in law.
  • Introduce provisional, emergency-use, and minor-use registration categories.
  • Enable parallel and equivalent registration for trusted jurisdictions.

Supply Chain & Manufacturing Incentives

Bangladesh's agro-input market suffers from fragmented supply chains, high costs, and weak distribution. Biopesticides often require better storage and shorter shelf-life management, making cold-chain and logistics investment essential.

Short-term

  • • Train local manufacturers in production and business models.
  • • Partner with distributors and cooperatives for last-mile access.
  • • Engage aggregators and market actors to build demand understanding.

Medium-term

  • • Standardize production protocols.
  • • Establish a national microbial biobank.
  • • Build climate-controlled storage and transport.
  • • Expand private-sector capacity through PPPs.

Farmer Adoption & Extension Services

Farmers often doubt biopesticide efficacy because action is slower and performance depends on environmental conditions. Higher costs and limited subsidies reduce adoption. Extension services are therefore central to behavior change.

Digital Extension

Scale e-Extension through SMS, IVR, mobile apps, farmer hotlines, and digital advisory platforms. Local digital champions and farmer field schools can demonstrate efficacy and build trust.

Economic Incentives: subsidies, input credit, demonstration farms, and premium market linkages can reduce adoption risk for smallholders.

Global Lessons: Learning from the World's Biopesticide Transitions

In 2025 the global biopesticides market reached $8.4 billion, projected to hit $26.1 billion by 2033 at a 15.3% CAGR. Yet adoption patterns differ sharply across regions. This chapter extracts actionable intelligence from seven comparator countries grouped into four clusters.

Cluster 1: Mature Regulatory Markets

🇧🇷

Brazil

Tripartite coordination (MAPA + ANVISA + IBAMA), "Priority Acts" for low-risk products, and the Sispa unified digital portal. 912 registrations approved in 2025; biologicals market grew from $833M (2025) toward $1.25B (2030).

📌 Lesson: Establish a Biopesticide Inter-Agency Task Force (DAE + Health + Environment) in Year 1 and a single digital application portal by Year 3.

🇪🇺

European Union

Farm to Fork targeted a 50% pesticide reduction by 2030 and achieved 58% by 2023 — but the Sustainable Use Regulation (SUR) was abandoned in February 2024 amid farmer protests and food-security fears.

📌 Lesson: Targets without substitution tools are political liabilities. Pair any reduction goal with guaranteed supply, subsidies, and price floors.

Cluster 2: ASEAN Peers

🇹🇭

Thailand

Fast-track registration cut to 6–12 months (vs 3 years standard); minimum-risk products exempted from toxicology tests (list grew from 5 to 17 products).

📌 Most transferable: replicate the 6–12 month fast-track and publish a minimum-risk exempt list within Year 1.

🇻🇳

Vietnam

ASEAN harmonization leader via ABETF; national regulations aligned with regional standards as a deliberate export-competitiveness strategy (MRL focus).

📌 Treat harmonization as a trade tool — align with ASEAN to meet MRLs for shrimp, tea, and vegetable exports.

🇵🇭

Philippines

CABI partnerships produced operational working manuals that translate high-level guidelines into step-by-step procedures for front-line reviewers.

📌 High-level policy needs operational manuals — build a "Biopesticide Reviewer's Handbook" by Year 2.

Cluster 3: Regional Neighbors

🇮🇳

India (Cautionary Tale)

970+ registered biopesticide companies and a $286M market (2025), yet only ~5% of pesticide use and ~11% farmer adoption. Solved supply but failed demand.

⚠ Warning: Regulatory reform alone cannot drive adoption. Commit 30%+ of the transition budget to farmer-facing interventions from Day 1.

🇨🇳

China

Fastest-growing market at 16.4% CAGR (outpacing the global 15.2%). Driven by top-level industrial policy redirecting chemical manufacturing toward biologicals.

📌 Frame biopesticide manufacturing as an industrial-development and export-diversification priority, not just agriculture.

Cluster 4: African Peers

🇹🇿

Tanzania (Model Reform)

Plant Health Act 2020 created three legal categories — synthetic, bio-pesticide, and biochemical — with stand-alone definitions. Registration tiers: full (5 yr), provisional (2 yr), experimental (1 yr).

📌 Most achievable structural reform: embed the three-category definition into Bangladesh's final Pesticide Rules within Year 1.

🇰🇪

Kenya

In 2025 withdrew 77 harmful pesticides and restricted 202 others, creating a demand vacuum biopesticides now fill. PCPB added modernized CRM registration requirements.

📌 Phase-outs stimulate demand — but only if alternatives are pre-registered. Sequence hazardous-pesticide withdrawal after fast-tracking substitutes.

Enhanced Phased Roadmap (Integrating Global Lessons)

Phase 1: Stabilization & Stimulation (Years 1–3)

  • Adopt Tanzania's three-category definition in the final Pesticide Rules (Year 1).
  • Launch a Thailand-style 6–12 month fast-track with toxicology-test exemptions within 18 months.
  • Produce a Philippines-style Reviewer's Handbook for DAE officers (Year 2).
  • Avoid India's trap: commit 30%+ of budget to demand-side interventions from Day 1.
  • Prepare Kenya-style phase-out sequencing of 10 highly hazardous pesticides (Year 3–4).

Phase 2: Building & Scaling (Years 4–10)

  • Establish a Brazil-style tripartite coordination mechanism + unified digital portal (Year 5).
  • Complete ASEAN harmonization via ABETF, focused on export-crop MRLs (Years 3–5).
  • Avoid EU target traps: pair any reduction goal with guaranteed supply and compensation.
  • Learn from China: frame biopesticide manufacturing as an industrial-development priority.
  • Build Kenya-style market pull via sequenced withdrawal of 25+ hazardous pesticides by Year 10.

Conclusion: Bangladesh's Unique Positioning

Bangladesh enters the transition at a historically favorable moment. Unlike India in 2010, it can leapfrog supply bottlenecks via Thailand's fast-track and Tanzania's definitions. Unlike the EU in 2020, it can avoid politically suicidal targets. Unlike Kenya in 2020, it can prepare phase-outs by building alternatives first. The strategic advantage is sequencing: compress a 15-year transition into 10 years by adopting proven architectures rather than inventing new ones.

Regulate like Tanzania, fast-track like Thailand, coordinate like Brazil, harmonize like Vietnam, avoid India's trap, and phase out like Kenya — all within a decade.
